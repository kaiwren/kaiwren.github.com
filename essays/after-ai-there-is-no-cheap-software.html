After AI, There Is No Cheap Software

Tweet Follow @ponnappa

26th October 2026 — I tweeted on 23 October that the era of good enough software is over. It is now the era of valuable enterprise software - software that has serious, tangible ROI, almost consumerish in its immediacy. Software that is worth the effort of keeping it secure and running in the face of AI-empowered attackers.

On the night of 11 October, the freezers in hundreds of French supermarkets turned into ovens. The manager of an Intermarché supermarket near Dijon got a call at about four in the morning, drove in and opened his freezers. “There was no more cold,” he told NPR, “just hot air coming out.” Someone had got into the controllers that run the refrigeration and kept the defrost cycle running for hours. Investigators think the alarms were switched off or delayed first, so by the time anyone was called, the food had thawed and started to cook.

Nobody needed AI for this one. The controllers sat on the open internet, and many were old models that Danfoss, the Danish company that makes them, had stopped supporting in 2016 and 2021, so there was no patch to apply. For years they were safe only because no attacker had bothered with them. AI means somebody is now looking at every system like that, all the time, and it costs them next to nothing.

The supermarkets’ fridge controllers were a facilities purchase, and most enterprises still run their software the same way - as a cost centre with its own department, bought as cheaply as they can get away with.

If your company refers to engineers as the IT department, you have nothing to worry about. You were already breached last week.

— Nikita Bier (@nikitabier) October 22, 2026

Software bought cheaply always came with a cleanup bill, the cost of finding its security holes and closing them, and enterprises never paid it, because nobody was looking.

Now somebody is.

A CIO who doesn’t pay the cleanup bill will pay for the breach instead. The French supermarkets are paying both: a night’s food that night, and new controllers in hundreds of stores now. Intermarché has already warned that its insurance may not cover the loss.

I thought AI would make software cheaper. It has made software cheaper to write, and I assumed the cost of owning it would fall too. In practice, AI is orders of magnitude better at finding the holes in software than at building software without them, so the cost of owning software is going up, and it goes up most for whoever built or bought it cheapest. That’s most of what enterprises run, and it was every one of those supermarkets.

In theory, any software a business bought always had to be worth what it cost. Now it has to deliver, and deliver the way consumer software is judged, in short cycles, with continuous impact that is objectively demonstrable. Mere claims of utility don’t cut it any more, because every system a business runs is somewhere an attacker can get in, and that liability is now expensive in a way it never was before.

After AI, there is no cheap software, there is only valuable software.

Enterprise Software Operates on Sales-Market Fit

Enterprise software has historically run on sales-market fit, not product-market fit. The person who signs for it is rarely the person who uses it, and whether it was worth the money gets decided in some nebulous way, quarters down the line. Product-market fit isn’t just about engagement, it’s about value, and enterprise software is not held to the same standards as consumer apps, where one mistake and the customer churns. B2B vendors only ever paid lip service to adoption, because once the contract was signed, whether anyone used the software was somebody else’s problem. A lot of what enterprises bought ended up as shelfware, renewed every year and barely used. Zylo, which sells SaaS-management software, measures licence use against what the industry recommends, and finds 36 percent of SaaS licences going unused.

Cheap software was never good software. Whether it was built in-house or bought, most of it was written by good-enough developers at the going rate for good-enough work, which in outsourced services is roughly $30 an hour. Code written that way has more defects and is harder to change. CodeScene, which sells code-quality tools, looked at 39 commercial codebases and found that the code its tool rated low had 15 times the defects of the code it rated high, and that fixing an issue took 124 percent longer on average. Enterprises paid for those defects in maintenance contracts and developer time every year the software stayed in use, and nobody added up what that came to. The CIO signs the licence renewals one at a time, each a line in that year’s budget and small enough to sign without anyone asking what the system has cost since the day it was bought. And what was the alternative, anyway?

Even where the core system is SAP or Oracle, most of what an enterprise runs is the software around it. There’s a vendor portal from 2014, the code three different contractors wrote to connect the systems to each other, a tool some business unit had built for itself, and the controllers that facilities bought for the buildings. That’s where the cheap software is, and most of it was signed for by people who never used it.

The defects in that software come in two kinds. The first kind is the one its users run into, like a change that takes months or a report that comes out wrong, and those are the defects the maintenance contracts paid for. Enterprises paid for that kind before go-live too, and NIST, the US government’s standards body, put finding and fixing defects at about 80 percent of the cost of developing software, back in 2002. The second kind is the security holes, which sit in the same code, written by the same good-enough developers, and because no user ever runs into one, no maintenance contract ever covered them. The cleanup bill is the cost of finding and closing that second kind, and it has been owed since the day the software went live.

Most Software Was Safe Because Nobody Was Looking

Enterprises did pay to find out whether their software could be broken. They hired a chief information security officer, usually reporting to the CIO, built a security team under them, and once a year or so brought in a pen test, where a few outside experts spent a few weeks trying to break into whichever systems the enterprise had chosen to put in scope. Synack, which sells penetration testing, paid for a survey of 200 security leaders at large US companies, and on average they were testing just 32 percent of what an attacker could reach.

That was enough, because of who was doing the attacking. Breaking into software took weeks of expert work, and the few who could pay for those weeks, mostly intelligence agencies and hostile states, spent them on targets worth the trouble. So software was as safe as the hours it would take to break it, and most enterprise software was never worth those hours. Jason Lemkin, who founded the SaaS conference SaaStr, quoted his former CTO on 23 October: “The only reason we haven’t been hacked is no one cares about us.” “True for so long,” Lemkin added.

If even a software company’s CTO believed nobody cared enough to break in, the CIO of a retailer or a bank certainly did. The pen test only had to show that the important systems held, and the CIO decided which systems those were and accepted the risk on everything else. For twenty years, almost nothing came of that decision.

The checks that were paid for missed things too. eMudhra, an Indian company that issues the certificates browsers use to trust websites, says a flaw in its keys “went undetected for about 18 months, through two independent audit cycles.” One of those keys, The Hindu reported, was just the number 4.

A Working Exploit Now Costs Twenty Dollars

Lemkin’s post had a third line: “No longer true for … anyone.” A day earlier, a programmer who posts on X as Dabs had pointed out that the work it took to break old software was what protected it, and that a machine now does that work.

a lot of old software was protected by “exploiting this would be an unreasonable amount of work”

we have invented a machine that does unreasonable amounts of work

anyway how’s the patch backlog

— Dabs (@DabsMalone) October 22, 2026

The machine is an AI model running in a loop, which guesses at a hole, writes code to get in through it, tries the code, and starts again if it fails. A machine can check for itself whether the code got in.

That code is called an exploit, and a working one now costs about twenty dollars. In September Anthropic, which makes the Claude models, reported that GLM-5.3, an open model from the Chinese lab Zhipu that anyone can download, built a working exploit for known flaws in Chrome in eight hours, with twenty minutes of help from a person, for $20.40 at Zhipu’s prices. Real attackers pay about the same. Gambit, a security firm, priced one campaign that broke into at least 27 companies at “$25.46 per completed target”. GreyNoise, a firm that tracks attacks across the internet, saw hundreds of AI agents break into 395 organisations through their print servers, and at one high school in the United States the attacker went from getting in to controlling the whole network in seven minutes. At those prices, a target no longer has to be worth anyone’s hours.

In October, the same kind of looking turned up holes that had sat unnoticed for years. eMudhra says the researcher who worked out its weak keys did it “reportedly with the help of AI-assisted tooling”. Researchers at the security firm Wiz used AI to turn old flaws in PHP into working attacks, and named them PHP2Shell. PHP is the programming language behind, in CNBC’s words, “just under 70% of all websites with known server-side programming languages”, WordPress and Wikipedia among them. One of the flaws goes back to a version of PHP released in 2012. CNBC reported that Matt Suiche’s team at the security firm Tolmo built a working exploit in less than a day, a job Suiche said would once have taken “multiple weeks”. The weeks of expert work that used to protect most software now take a day.

A yearly pen test checks the systems in scope for a few weeks, while an attacker’s loop can search everything an enterprise has on the internet, all year, and start again every time a better model comes out. Some of what it finds is software the CIO never sees. The security consultancy hard2bit points out that fridge controllers like the ones in France usually sit outside the IT inventory, because “facilities or construction buys it, a third party installs and maintains it”. A pen test rarely has them in scope. A system doesn’t even need to be on the internet, as long as a vendor has a login to it. On 22 October TechCrunch reported, citing Microsoft, that attackers who had broken into ConnectWise, an IT-management company, stole the credentials that link it to its customers’ systems and then used AI agents to sign in to around 2,250 organisations, at almost 3,000 attempted sign-ins a minute.

Nobody gets a warning first, either. Mandiant, the security firm Google owns, estimates that attackers now exploit a hole, on average, about seven days before a patch for it exists. Amazon’s security team, writing about PHP2Shell, told customers that a deadline of seven or thirty days to apply a patch “assumes a delay that no longer exists”. When PHP2Shell went public, the security firm Artemis saw an attacker using a weaponised copy of the first published exploit within 30 minutes. The yearly pen test and the patch both arrive after the attacker does.

Cheaply Built Software Has the Most Holes to Close

Everyone agrees that defending against all this is going to cost a lot. Aaron Levie, who runs the cloud-storage company Box, wrote on 23 October that “years and years of legacy enterprise infrastructure will need to be upgraded”. He’s right, and he leaves two things out. The first is why the cost can’t be brought down with the same AI models that find the holes, and the second is what decided how big that cost is for any one enterprise.

An exploit either works or it doesn’t, which is why a machine can search for holes at scale for tens of dollars. Closing a hole often means changing how the system is built, and no machine can check a design the way it checks an exploit. There’s no test that tells you whether the part of a program that shows data is properly kept apart from the part that stores it, and that is the kind of judgement good architecture is made of. So closing holes still runs on people. The models that find the holes don’t make that part cheap.

Greg Brockman, OpenAI’s president, says the company “took 25% of our production engineers and said, ‘Sorry, all your projects are on hold. You are now defending.’” The models were there “to find all the holes”, and the engineers were there for what he called “up-leveling our security architecture”. The US National Academies of Sciences calls fixing “a more complex and resource-intensive process” than exploiting, “even when the same underlying vulnerability is at issue”. A tool can apply a patch that exists, but it can’t redesign a system whose hole is in its architecture, and for the French fridge controllers there was no patch at all.

The size of the bill for any one enterprise was decided by how well its software was built, whether its own team built it or a vendor did. Security is a requirement no user ever sees, so it was the first thing a cheap build left out. Wiz traced the PHP2Shell flaws to “key architecture decisions, some made over a decade ago”. PHP is some of the most looked-at code in the world, and it has a foundation behind it. The PHP Foundation started on the problem behind one of the flaws in 2024, and has since called the delay a “structural failure, caused by both the security team’s decision process and its lack of resources”. The vendor portal from 2014 has nobody working on it at all.

An attacker needs only one hole. The defender has to close all of them, and only the defender pays to fix. Maintenance was a bill an enterprise could pay at its own pace, a little every year. The cleanup bill can also be paid one system at a time, but only until someone finds a hole in a system you haven’t got to yet, and from then on you’re paying for the breach. In France the breach came in one night, and controllers bought to keep the food cold were used to cook it.

Validating Software Will Cost More Than Writing It

The cleanup bill lands on the CIO, and mostly on the engineering budget rather than the security team’s, because closing holes is engineering work. Enterprises always spent most of what it cost to build software on one kind of checking, finding and fixing defects, which is NIST’s 80 percent. Now the kind they only ever sampled, whether the software can be broken, goes on top. The kind they paid for already cost more than writing the code, and the kind they sampled is the bigger of the two, because it has to be done again every time attackers get a better model. Meanwhile AI keeps making the writing cheaper.

Finding any one hole gets cheaper with every model, too. But every change anyone makes to the code now has to be checked for holes before it goes live. Every time a better model comes out, everything already running has to be checked again, because the new model finds holes the old one missed. OpenAI says it spent “hundreds of billions of tokens”, the units AI models are billed in, attacking its own systems to find the holes first. As Brockman put it, “there will be a new model, there will be a new round.”

Anthropic said in May that security “used to be limited by how quickly we could find new vulnerabilities. Now it’s limited by how quickly we can verify, disclose, and patch” them. In six months of scanning widely used open-source software, its models turned up more than 29,000 possible holes, outside firms had worked through about 6,000 of them, and only 516 had been fixed, The New Stack reported in October. Several of the people who maintain that software told Anthropic they were “severely capacity constrained”. Inside an enterprise, the work starts before any fix, with finding out what is running and who owns it. A parody account that writes as an enterprise IT manager summed up the PHP2Shell patch in a thread 58,000 people liked: “The emergency patch took four hours. Finding out what PHP actually runs took eleven.” Budget and people are both scarce, and people who can fix architecture are the scarcer of the two.

The other way to pay is the breach, which IBM puts at a global average of $4.99 million. Regulators have started counting in hours and days: the EU wants an early warning within 24 hours from makers whose products have a hole attackers are already using, and America’s SEC wants listed companies to disclose a material breach within four business days. India’s data-protection rules, which phase in by 2027, set penalties of up to ₹250 crore, 2.5 billion rupees, for failing to keep data safe. eMudhra announced five days after its weak keys were reported that it was leaving the global certificate business, citing compliance and cost, and The Hindu reported its shares fell nearly 20 percent on the day the government announced a probe. Paying for the breach doesn’t get anyone out of the cleanup, because the holes are still there afterwards.

Nobody has published how much validating software costs compared with writing it, and I’m not going to make up a multiple. An enterprise that built or bought its software on a shoestring now faces a cleanup bill far bigger than anything it planned for, and the bill runs again with every new model. The real number for any one enterprise comes from going through what it runs, one system at a time, and that is the CIO’s job from Monday morning.

Cheap Software Comes After the Cleanup

Most enterprises treated software as a cost centre, and that is what ran up the cleanup bill. A cost centre is judged on what it spends this year, so every system went to the cheapest bidder who could make it work, and the holes landed on a bill nobody was ever going to send. The security writer Bruce Schneier calls this “the problem of it being cheaper to be vulnerable than to be secure.” AI hasn’t changed what that software cost to build, but it has put a price on being vulnerable, and a cost-centre budget has no line for it.

So software can’t be judged on what it costs any more, only on what it delivers, the way consumer software always was. A consumer app is judged by the dopamine that keeps people opening it, and enterprise software against budgets, KPIs and the promotions of the people who sign for it. In When the Models Get Better, Your TAM Should Go Up I said those consumer dynamics were already entering the enterprise. The cost of defending every system is what forces them in now.

Software gets cheap again only after two things happen in parallel. What an enterprise already runs has to be rebuilt or retired, and new software has to be built by people who can make it cheap to validate. Doing only one of them doesn’t bring the cost down, because new systems built cheaply run up a new bill, and building them well does nothing for the vendor portal from 2014.

The obvious objection is that AI is making software so cheap to write that there will be far more of it, whatever it costs to defend. In Don’t Go AI Native I argued that when something gets dramatically cheaper to produce you get vastly more of it, and that is happening to software now. But every new system is one more thing to defend. After AI, There Is No Product made the other half of the case, that once software is cheap to build, what an enterprise holds is inventory rather than an asset. Now that inventory has to be defended as well. Schneier’s long-term bet is that AI will help defenders more than attackers, but in the short term, he says, “attackers benefit more because existing legacy code is vulnerable.” Anthropic calls the time until then “this interim period”, while holes are “rapidly discovered and slowly patched”, and it lasts for as long as cheaply built software is still running.

If I were the CIO, this is what I’d do on Monday.

  • Retire the shelfware first. Nobody uses it, and it still has to be defended.
  • Ask every vendor how they check their software for holes, and price the answer into the renewal, because the holes in bought software are on the CIO’s bill too.
  • Go through what the enterprise runs, including what facilities bought, one system at a time, work out what checking each one would cost, and decide whether to rebuild it, retire it or defend it. Retiring a system is the cheapest way there is to stop paying for its holes.
  • Stop paying $30 an hour for good-enough work, whoever it comes from. I’ve argued why before, and the cleanup bill is one more reason.

Once the cleanup is done and the people building software can make it cheap to validate, software will get cheap again. Until then, there is no cheap software, there is only valuable software.
