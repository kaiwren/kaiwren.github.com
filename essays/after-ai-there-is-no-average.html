After AI, There Is No Average

Tweet Follow @ponnappa

9th October 2026 — I was on Zerodha’s Subtext recently, talking to Pranav and Bhuvan about what an AI-native firm is, and the part people kept DMing me about afterwards was the few minutes on the middle tier of talent disappearing. This essay is the longer answer. The situation is changing every quarter, as enterprise attempts at AI adoption play out and the models underneath them improve, so read it as where things stand in October 2026.

The enterprise playbook treats AI as a rising tide that lifts the median employee to where the best one used to be and turns the best into small gods. But an agent amplifies whoever is using it, so good judgement produces superhuman results, and poor judgement, slop. Some of the people an enterprise rates average have that judgement, and with an agent in hand they prove themselves exceptional.

Nobody using AI remains merely average.

The World Runs on Good Enough

So far, human endeavour has scaled on the back of a simple principle: in order to do more work, we need more people. And since exceptional talent is, by definition, exceptional, all economically viable scaling is based on a second, equally important principle: the organization must be scaled such that most of it consists of good enough people doing good enough work. It is the essential difference between a small startup and a large enterprise.

In practice, every organization at scale runs on one rule: exceptional talent for the important problems, good-enough talent for the routine ones, and a steady churn for the bad. And managers routinely make the call that holds it all up: for this problem, for this team, for this workflow, for this output, I am okay with average talent and average results. Education systems have evolved to supply that demand in volume, and while some graduates rise above average and a few well above, the bulk of every graduating class sits squarely on good enough. Most of them have never had a job that asked for more.

In outsourced services, good enough has a price: $30 an hour, plus a certain amount of management, buys an adequate outcome. Services firms quote that price openly, so it is the reference price for average work everywhere. Every enterprise in the world pays its own version of it to its in-house middle, in salaries rather than invoices. The org chart, the pay bands and the review cycle all rest on the middle of the bell curve delivering that outcome at that price, year after year.

Agents Magnify Their Human

Now, however, the enterprise built on that bell curve has set out to become AI-native. As it sees things, that means putting AI alongside each of its people to do the work. Taste, judgement and operating discipline remain the responsibility of whoever the model is working for, and will for as long as models glaze their users, telling them their work is good.

Whatever that person brings to the work - their intent, their standards, their blind spots - the agent builds into everything it produces, at a speed and volume no colleague could match. An agent is you, and it is only as good as you are. So in setting out to become AI-native, an enterprise stakes the quality of all its work on the judgement of every one of its people, including the good-enough middle it was built on.

Median Judgement Runs Out Before Lunch

As things stand today, the effective use of AI requires there be a human in the loop. And this human in the loop needs to have a high capacity for quality decision making, because if you and your agent do a month’s worth of work in a day, you now have to make a month’s worth of decisions in a day.

And this, it turns out, is not a majority trait in the workforce. When faced with having to make nuanced, thoughtful and consequential decisions several times an hour, most folk start to fade.

The second, compounding, factor is self-awareness. AI agents aim to please the human in their loop and in the process, magnify every bias in that human’s judgement. As a role, human-in-the-loop demands self-awareness paired with a skeptic’s mindset. This is exactly the kind of mindset that craftspeople serious about elevating their craft cultivate.

And so, when you arm a human who is low decision-making stamina, or low self-awareness or low craft with an agent… you get slop.

An hour with an agent can spend a whole day’s worth of decision-making stamina, so the same person who does their best work at ten in the morning can be waving slop through by noon. The good-enough middle gets there first and most often, because by and large it lacks the discipline to keep passing judgement at that pace, and it stops judging long before it stops using the agent.

The best people build systems that make good work happen sustainably, rather than holding up exceptional performance through brute effort alone. They introspect until they can name their own craft. Then they externalise it into the standards, checks and workflows an agent can follow, so their judgement goes only where it counts. In the middle, that ability is almost always missing, and once they adopt AI, the few who build it rocket up through the organization.

Every bit of slop waved through compounds, the next day and the day after, eating at the integrity of the system until the system can no longer do its job. In marketing, each campaign drifts a little further off the brand pillars, every one of them waved through because the human was too tired to demand better, until the brand stands for nothing in particular. In finance, one unchecked assumption in a model gets copied into every plan built on it, and in sales, a proposal with a delivery date nobody checked with the team becomes the template for the next ten. We used to call this technical debt, but it is not technical anymore, it is just debt.

Back then, this was the curve of tech companies that had chosen speed as their entire edge and paid for talent to match, while everyone else could still run on good enough. Agents put everyone else on that curve.

Adding AI to an organization breaks the HR bell curve and polarizes it into a power law: exceptional talent become 100x more productive and impactful, average talent become slop cannons. And we have watched some of last year’s average performers land at the top of that power law, running portfolios several times their old size.

Nobody Pays for Good Enough

The $30 an hour that used to buy an adequate outcome no longer does, because good-enough judgement with an agent in hand turns out slop at volume. That slop compounds into debt, which the employer pays down in rework on everything built on top of it. Employers now either get trivial prototypes out of an agent for next to nothing, or fight OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and the who’s who of the industry to hire the few people who can be trusted with an agent to build anything that has to keep working, quarter after quarter.

For two and a half years, our mission at realfast was to supercharge India’s median export, the roughly $30-an-hour services work it sells by the hundreds of billions of dollars a year. Then we made a U-turn. There is nothing we can do at that price point. Our game now is ninety-ninth percentile talent, and nothing else.

Conviction Is Earned First Hand

From the board, to the C-suite, to middle management and on to the juniormost IC, most of the average talent flips from doing acceptable work to ruining everything they touch.

At the top, the board’s job is to drive the AI transformation itself. The board has to believe that average performers get worse with AI, and that comp bands have to go up fourfold over time, when everything it knows about scaling an organization says the opposite. A report won’t persuade a board of that.

Driving the transformation is a change management problem of epic proportions, which takes will to see through. Will comes from conviction. Boards usually follow other enterprises that have gone first, and with agents, only the frontier labs have. Big tech is trying, and Meta has already walked part of its reorg back. No board is going to redraw its org chart because Meta did, or because an HBS paper says so. It hands its people agents and calls that the transformation.

A board member who uses an agent themselves does a week’s worth of their own work in a day, and stops asking whether any of this is real. Use one seriously, and you also find out how fast your own judgement runs out at that pace. The good-enough middle, with less decision-making stamina to spend, runs out well before lunch.

A push from the board and the C-suite is enough to start an AI rollout, but not to see it through. The first quarter brings real spend, no proof and the incredible pushback AI generates, and only board members who have used an agent themselves keep driving in the right direction.

It’s Not a Skill Issue

Any enterprise scale AI transformation strategy has to solve for slop. And solving for slop is a HR problem, not a tech problem.

The usual HR answer to a capability gap is a training programme, but you can’t train people to be productive with AI. You learn about AI by asking the AI to teach you about AI, which seems so simple and is so hard. No course can hand anyone the decision-making stamina, self-awareness or craft that takes. The people who get there teach themselves, so an enterprise can only hire for it, and some of them already work there.

few seem to understand that AI slop is a problem caused by mediocre humans, not mediocre AI

— Sidu Ponnappa (@ponnappa) July 29, 2026

To hire for it and keep the people you hire, you have to 4x the comp bands, because those bands were cut for a bell curve and the people you need are the ones OpenAI, Anthropic and Google are bidding for. You have to drop review cycles to one quarter, because slop compounds by the day, and the quarter after a rollout is when you need to see whether it is working. And you have to move this work out into a new management structure that reports directly to the CEO and the board. No team becomes AI-native inside the hierarchy, comp bands and review process built for the bell curve, and the board is where the transformation has to be driven from.

Enterprises are going to struggle with adapting their HR.

Their best people have gone from 2X potential impact to 100X, and the biggest blockers to delivering that 100X are everyone else in the org.

The evolution of the enterprise performance review process is the bottleneck.

— Sidu Ponnappa (@ponnappa) June 12, 2026

An enterprise that puts an agent alongside every one of its people gets back whatever those people bring, magnified, so its AI transformation will succeed or fail on who it hires, how often it reviews them and what it pays them.
